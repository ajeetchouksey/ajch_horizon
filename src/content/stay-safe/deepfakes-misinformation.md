A "deepfake" is a fake photo, video, or audio clip made by AI to look or sound like a real person doing or saying something they never actually did. As image and voice generators get better, these fakes get harder to spot with just a quick glance.

## Why this matters

You already know AI image generators work by learning patterns from millions of real images. That same pattern-learning can be pointed at making a fake image of a real, specific person look convincing. The same goes for AI voice tools that can learn the pattern of how someone talks and generate new audio that sounds like them saying something they never said.

This isn't just about photos of celebrities — misinformation (false information that spreads, sometimes on purpose) can use these same tricks to make a fake news story or fake quote look real and trustworthy.

## A real-world example

Imagine a video clip showing a person appearing to say something shocking or embarrassing. If you look closely, deepfakes often still have "tells" — mistakes the pattern-guessing gets wrong:

- **Hands and fingers** — often too many, too few, or oddly shaped.
- **Blurry or warped backgrounds**, especially where the background meets the person.
- **Lighting that doesn't quite match** between the person and their surroundings.
- **Unnatural blinking**, or a mouth that doesn't perfectly match the words.
- Skin or hair that looks a little too smooth or "too perfect."

None of these tells are guaranteed — AI keeps getting better — which is why the habit matters more than any specific trick.

## What to do

- **Pause before you believe or share.** That's Golden Rule #3 for a reason — a surprising or shocking video is exactly the kind of content worth double-checking first.
- **Check if trustworthy news sources are reporting the same thing.** If something huge happened, it won't be reported by just one random account.
- **Ask a grown-up** if something looks or sounds suspicious, especially before sharing it further.
- Remember: just because something *looks* real doesn't mean it *is* real. "Don't trust — verify" applies here more than almost anywhere else.
