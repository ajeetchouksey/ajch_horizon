AI is really good at writing convincing, natural-sounding text — which is exactly what makes it useful for scammers too. It's now much easier for someone to fake a believable message than it used to be.

## Why this is a bigger deal now

A few years ago, scam messages were often easy to spot — bad spelling, weird grammar, obviously fake logos. AI tools can now write a scam message that sounds just as polished and personal as a real one from a friend, a company, or even a game you play. The "it sounds professional so it must be real" trick doesn't work as well as it used to.

## A real-world example

Imagine getting a message that looks like it's from a game you play, saying "You've won a rare item — click here to claim it before it expires!" It uses the game's real logo, mentions details that sound accurate, and creates urgency ("before it expires!") so you click without thinking. That combination — a prize, urgency, and a link — is one of the oldest scam patterns there is, just written more convincingly by AI now.

Fake friend requests work the same way: a message that sounds like it's really from a classmate, asking you to click a link or share a code, when it's actually not them at all.

## What to do

- **Slow down.** Scams work by rushing you. If a message is pushing you to act *right now*, that urgency is itself a warning sign.
- **Don't click links or share codes** from messages you weren't expecting, even if they look like they're from someone you know.
- **Verify a different way.** If a "friend" messages you something unusual, ask them about it in person or through a different app before doing anything.
- **Tell a grown-up.** If anything feels off — too good to be true, oddly urgent, asking for personal info — that's exactly what Golden Rule #4 is for: ask a parent or teacher.
